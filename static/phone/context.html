Five short guides for a claim, caption, quotation, number or endorsement. Choose a question below and open its checks. This page does not decide what is true.

Review an ad or endorsement →

Sources: are they independent?

Trace the evidence behind each account.

  1. Find the original account. Read the full report, notice or statement, including qualifications and corrections.
  2. Follow what each source relies on. Different websites may repeat the same witness, press release or image. Website count is not evidence of independence.
  3. Look for relevant contrary evidence. Ask what would change your view. Supporting and challenging accounts do not automatically deserve equal weight.

Constructed example

Two websites repeat one press release. That is not two independent confirmations. Trace the release and what evidence each account adds; the shared source alone does not make the claim true or false.

These are review actions, not a credibility score. No source is opened or fetched by this guide.

Images: what does the caption claim?

Separate the file from the story.

  1. Find the earliest full caption. Check what the account actually says about the place, event and people, rather than relying on a crop or repost.
  2. Compare dates separately. A file clock, publication date and event date can describe different things. Metadata may be edited, copied or removed.
  3. Keep the scene unresolved when evidence is missing. Seek context beyond the image. A file label or missing metadata does not prove authenticity, AI generation or a caption’s accuracy.

Constructed example

A repost says “today’s storm,” while an editable file field has an older date. Find the original account and event context. The file date alone does not settle whether the caption is accurate.

This guide does not receive an image, inspect metadata, locate a scene or run an origin model. Signed provenance can bind a declaration to a file; it does not verify the caption or depicted event.

Quotations: what wording changed?

Read the qualification and its context.

  1. Find the fuller account. Read the words before and after the quotation, including the question being answered.
  2. Compare the exact wording. Notice additions, omissions, punctuation and qualifications. Matching text does not authenticate a source.
  3. Separate observation from interpretation. A literal change is something to examine; it does not establish meaning, intent or which version is factually correct.

Constructed example

One supplied version says “The site may close for repairs.” Another says “The site will close permanently.” The wording differs. Read the full source before interpreting the change; neither supplied version is authenticated here.

This page does not compare pasted text or verify a speaker. Both versions in the example are constructed and unverified.

Numbers: what is the count out of?

Check the denominator before the claim.

  1. Identify the count and total. Ask who or what was included, excluded and measured within each group.
  2. Compare proportions as well as counts. Use the same definitions. A larger raw count can represent a smaller share when the totals differ.
  3. Check comparability before explaining a difference. Groups may differ in selection, time period or circumstances. A rate difference alone establishes neither statistical significance nor cause.

Constructed example

Group A: 20 of 100, or 20%. Group B: 30 of 200, or 15%. Group B has the larger count but the smaller proportion. Group A’s rate is 5 percentage points higher than Group B’s.

This is arithmetic on constructed counts, not a study result. It does not establish data quality, significance, causality or whether a claim is true. Group A and Group B imply no time order.

Ads: what supports the endorsement?

Look beyond the recommendation.

  1. Identify the source. Ask what you know about the reviewer and platform. Look for payment, free-product or affiliate disclosures.
  2. Compare several sources. Consider relevant independent assessments, not only a seller’s chosen reviews.
  3. Separate evidence from appearance. Writing style cannot reliably establish whether a review is genuine. A disclosed incentive does not by itself settle the product claim.

Constructed example

A sponsored headphone post promises “twice the battery life” without naming another model or settings. Find its comparison method and relevant tests before treating the claim as a measurement.

This guide does not detect fake reviews or decide whether a product is worth buying. It gives fixed review actions, not a legal-compliance finding.
